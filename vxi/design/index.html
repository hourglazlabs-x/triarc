  1. 01

    Filippo Brunelleschi

    1377–1446 · Early Renaissance · Architect, engineer, sculptor, goldsmith

    A Florentine architect and engineer, one of the pioneers of early Renaissance architecture in Italy. Trained as a goldsmith and sculptor, he is best known for the dome of Florence Cathedral (1420–1436), which he showed could be built without a traditional wooden framework. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    Life & times

    • Born in Florence in 1377; died there on 15 April 1446, and buried in the cathedral. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • He trained as a goldsmith and sculptor, and was designated a master in 1401. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • In 1401 he was one of seven competitors for a second bronze door for the Florence Baptistery; Lorenzo Ghiberti won it. Sourced[1] Sacrifice of Isaac | Brunelleschi, Filippo | V&A Explore The Collections, Victoria and Albert Museum (opens in a new tab)[2] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • Around 1401 he is believed to have travelled to Rome with his friend Donatello to study classical sculpture and architecture. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • In 1418 the cathedral’s authorities offered a prize for models of devices to build the dome; he, Ghiberti and many others submitted them. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • Building began on 7 August 1420, with a banquet of melons and wine; only about sixty master builders worked on the dome under his direction. Sourced[1] August 7, 1420: The start of construction of the dome, Opera di Santa Maria del Fiore (Opera magazine) (opens in a new tab)[2] The construction site of Brunelleschi's Dome: men and machines, Opera di Santa Maria del Fiore (Opera magazine) (opens in a new tab)
    • He left no drawings of his building machines; they are known from drawings by others, among them the young Leonardo. Sourced[1] The dome worksite, Museo Galileo – Institute and Museum of the History of Science (opens in a new tab)
    • The lantern on top of his dome was completed only after his death. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    Principles

    1. Two shells. The dome is built as two vaults, an inner and an outer shell, one supporting the other. Sourced[1] August 7, 1420: The start of construction of the dome, Opera di Santa Maria del Fiore (Opera magazine) (opens in a new tab)
    2. Herringbone brickwork. Bricks were laid in herringbone patterns between a framework of stone beams: part of the method that let the dome rise without a wooden framework. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    3. Built without centring. No centring, the temporary wooden or iron structure that would otherwise hold up masonry while it is laid, was used to build the dome. Sourced[1] The Dome of Santa Maria del Fiore, Museo Galileo – Institute and Museum of the History of Science (opens in a new tab)
    4. Hidden chains. Hidden chains run round the dome and reinforce the structure. Sourced[1] August 7, 1420: The start of construction of the dome, Opera di Santa Maria del Fiore (Opera magazine) (opens in a new tab)
    5. The ox hoist. Around 1420 he designed a hoist turned by a pair of oxen; its gears could reverse, so loads went up or down without unyoking the animals. Sourced[1] Three-speed winch - Museo Leonardiano di Vinci, Museo Leonardiano di Vinci (opens in a new tab)
    6. Linear perspective. He rediscovered the principles of linear perspective known to the Greeks and Romans, and demonstrated them with two painted panels of Florentine streets, now lost. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    Works

    • 1420–1436 · Dome of Santa Maria del Fiore · FlorenceIn the hologram
      An octagonal double-shell dome of stone and brick, which the cathedral’s works office describes as still the largest masonry vault in the world. Sourced[1] Dome, Opera di Santa Maria del Fiore (opens in a new tab)
    • 1419–1444 · Ospedale degli Innocenti · Florence
      A hospital commissioned by the Arte della Seta and built under his direction; it now houses a museum. Sourced[1] Ospedale degli Innocenti - Himetop, Himetop, Università Campus Bio-Medico di Roma (opens in a new tab)[2] Museo degli Innocenti | Visit Tuscany, Visit Tuscany (opens in a new tab)
    • Begun 1421 · Old Sacristy, San Lorenzo · Florence
      A sacristy commissioned by the Medici family, completed, without its decoration, by 1428. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • c. 1429 · Pazzi Chapel · Santa Croce, Florence
      A chapel beside the church of Santa Croce, commissioned by the Pazzi family; a hemispherical dome covers its central square. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • 1428 or 1434 · Santo Spirito · Florence
      A basilican church with a centrally planned east end; after his death its protruding round chapels were walled over. Sourced[1] Filippo Brunelleschi, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • 1401 · The Sacrifice of Isaac · Bargello, Florence
      His gilt bronze trial relief for the Baptistery door competition; the V&A holds an electrotype copy made around 1871. Sourced[1] Sacrifice of Isaac | Brunelleschi, Filippo | V&A Explore The Collections, Victoria and Albert Museum (opens in a new tab)

    The hologram

    The dome of Santa Maria del Fiore as built, a quarter cut away. Its two shells, ribs, chains and herringbone courses follow the sources; their thicknesses and the lantern are illustrative, and the ox hoist beside it is simplified and enlarged. Not to scale.

  2. 02

    Joseph Paxton

    1803–1865 · Victorian · Gardener, glasshouse designer, architect, MP

    An English landscape gardener and designer of hothouses who rose from gardener to the Duke of Devonshire to architect of the Crystal Palace, his building of prefabricated sheet glass and iron for the Great Exhibition of 1851. Sourced[1] Sir Joseph Paxton, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    Life & times

    • Born at Milton Bryan, Bedfordshire, on 3 August 1803 (Britannica gives 1801); died at Sydenham, south London, on 8 June 1865. Sourced[1] Joseph Paxton | Science Museum Group Collection, Science Museum Group (opens in a new tab)[2] Sir Joseph Paxton, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • He was the youngest of eight children of William Paxton, an agricultural labourer, and his wife Ann Rooke. Sourced[1] Joseph Paxton | Science Museum Group Collection, Science Museum Group (opens in a new tab)
    • In 1826 the Duke of Devonshire took him on as head gardener at Chatsworth, in Derbyshire. Sourced[1] Joseph Paxton | Science Museum Group Collection, Science Museum Group (opens in a new tab)
    • By his own account the giant water lily Victoria regia, in a glasshouse he fitted up for it, flowered on 8 November 1849: the first time in the country. Sourced[1] The Industrial Palace in Hyde-Park. Mr. Paxton's Lecture, at the Society of Arts, The Victorian Web (transcription of The Illustrated London News, 16 November 1850) (opens in a new tab)
    • In June 1850 he drew his first sketches for the Great Exhibition building during a railway board meeting in Derby, on the lines of his Lily House at Chatsworth. Sourced[1] The Great Exhibition building | Paxton, Joseph (Sir) | V&A Explore The Collections, Victoria and Albert Museum (opens in a new tab)
    • The Great Exhibition opened in the Crystal Palace on 1 May 1851. Its success made him famous, and he was knighted that year. Sourced[1] Crystal Palace, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)[2] Joseph Paxton | Science Museum Group Collection, Science Museum Group (opens in a new tab)
    • He laid out Birkenhead Park, opened in 1847 as the first public park in the UK made at public expense; it influenced Olmsted’s Central Park. Sourced[1] Birkenhead Park, Historic England (opens in a new tab)
    • From 1854 until his death he was Member of Parliament for Coventry. Sourced[1] Sir Joseph Paxton, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    “My plans were got up in about ten days”

    His lecture to the Society of Arts, printed in The Illustrated London News, 16 November 1850.

    Principles

    1. Prefabrication. The Crystal Palace was built from prefabricated parts: a network of slender iron members holding walls of clear glass. Sourced[1] Crystal Palace, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    2. A standard module. It was planned as a modular design, from prefabricated components of standard sizes; no single part was more than 24 ft long. Sourced[1] A to Z of Expo architects: Joseph Paxton, Bureau International des Expositions (BIE) (opens in a new tab)[2] Exhibiting international industry, King's College London (King's Collections online exhibition) (opens in a new tab)
    3. Ridge and furrow. He set the roof’s panes in ridges and furrows, so low morning and evening sun came in unobstructed: a glasshouse idea, used at the scale of a hall. Sourced[1] The Industrial Palace in Hyde-Park. Mr. Paxton's Lecture, at the Society of Arts, The Victorian Web (transcription of The Illustrated London News, 16 November 1850) (opens in a new tab)
    4. Speed. With time short before the exhibition, building in glass and iron from prefabricated sections meant the hall could go up quickly. Sourced[1] The Great Exhibition building | Paxton, Joseph (Sir) | V&A Explore The Collections, Victoria and Albert Museum (opens in a new tab)
    5. The lily leaf. Kew sent him one of its giant water lilies; the plant’s leaves, ribbed underneath, are said to have inspired the design. Sourced[1] Giant waterlily, Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew (opens in a new tab)
    6. Drainage in the frame. Rain ran along the roof gutters into transverse gutters, then down through the hollow columns, which served as drainpipes. Sourced[1] The Industrial Palace in Hyde-Park. Mr. Paxton's Lecture, at the Society of Arts, The Victorian Web (transcription of The Illustrated London News, 16 November 1850) (opens in a new tab)

    Works

    • Completed 1841 · Great Conservatory, Chatsworth · Derbyshire
      A glasshouse nearly 100 m long and over 60 ft high, glazed with nearly 70,000 sq ft of glass; dismantled in 1920. Sourced[1] Chatsworth's Great Conservatory: The Glasshouse That Changed the World, Chatsworth House (opens in a new tab)
    • 1844 · Emperor Fountain · Chatsworth, Derbyshire
      Fed by water falling 122 m through an iron pipe; recorded at 90 m high, it has reached 60 m since new nozzles were fitted in 2014. Sourced[1] Emperor Fountain, Chatsworth House (opens in a new tab)
    • 1850 · First sketch for the Great Exhibition building · Now in the V&A, London
      Pen and ink on pink blotting paper, mounted with a telegram confirming that the plan was accepted. Sourced[1] The Great Exhibition building | Paxton, Joseph (Sir) | V&A Explore The Collections, Victoria and Albert Museum (opens in a new tab)
    • 1851 · The Crystal Palace, Hyde Park · LondonIn the hologram
      The prefabricated iron-and-glass hall of the Great Exhibition, which opened in it on 1 May 1851. Sourced[1] Crystal Palace, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • 1852–1854 · The Crystal Palace, Sydenham · London
      Taken down in Hyde Park and rebuilt on Sydenham Hill, where fire virtually destroyed it on the night of 30 November 1936. Sourced[1] Crystal Palace, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • 1852–1854 · Mentmore Towers · Buckinghamshire
      A country house in the Jacobethan style, built with G. H. Stokes for Baron Mayer Amschel de Rothschild. Sourced[1] Mentmore Towers, Historic England (opens in a new tab)

    The hologram

    The Crystal Palace in Hyde Park: the crossing of nave and transept, and a few of its 24 ft bays. The width, aisles, vault and elms follow the sources; the tier heights are illustrative, and the lily leaf in front is much enlarged. Not to scale.

  3. 03

    Antoni Gaudí

    1852–1926 · Modernisme · Architect, designer

    An architect whose works in and around Barcelona are particularly associated with Modernisme. UNESCO describes them as an exceptional creative contribution to the development of architecture and construction technology in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Sourced[1] Works of Antoni Gaudí, UNESCO World Heritage Centre (opens in a new tab)

    Life & times

    • Born in Reus, Catalonia, on 25 June 1852; died in Barcelona on 10 June 1926. Sourced[1] Gaudi obra - Antoni Gaudí - Sagrada Família, Basílica de la Sagrada Família (official website) (opens in a new tab)[2] Antoni Gaudí, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • He received his architecture degree on 15 March 1878; five years later he became the architect of the Sagrada Família. Sourced[1] The Sagrada Família, a repository of other Gaudí projects, Blog Sagrada Família (Basílica de la Sagrada Família) (opens in a new tab)
    • His main client was Eusebi Güell, who commissioned the Palau Güell as his house in the centre of Barcelona. Sourced[1] Gaudí’s Palau Güell in Barcelona, Fundació Catalunya La Pedrera (La Pedrera-Casa Milà official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • He took over the Sagrada Família in 1883, aged 31, and gave the last twelve years of his life to it alone. Sourced[1] Gaudi obra - Antoni Gaudí - Sagrada Família, Basílica de la Sagrada Família (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • Of the eighteen towers he designed for it, only one, the tower of Barnabas, was finished in his lifetime, in 1925. Sourced[1] Sagrada Família hosts commemoration of hundredth anniversary of Barnabas tower, Basílica de la Sagrada Família (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • He died on 10 June 1926 after being run over by a tram, and is buried in the crypt of the Sagrada Família. Sourced[1] Gaudi obra - Antoni Gaudí - Sagrada Família, Basílica de la Sagrada Família (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • In 1936, during the Spanish Civil War, the Sagrada Família was vandalised: its plans and photographs were burnt and its plaster models smashed. Sourced[1] History of the Temple - Sagrada Família, Basílica de la Sagrada Família (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • On 20 February 2026 the upper arm of the cross was placed on its central tower, completing the tower’s exterior at 172.5 metres. Sourced[1] Cross lands in sky above Barcelona, Basílica de la Sagrada Família (official website) (opens in a new tab)

    “Originality consists in returning to the origin”

    A saying attributed to him on the Casa Batlló’s official site, with no date or original document.

    Principles

    1. Balanced arches. He used catenary arches, which he called ‘balanced’ arches: light, easy to build, and standing on their own without buttresses. Sourced[1] Architecture of Gaudí’s La Pedrera | Casa Milà Official website, Fundació Catalunya La Pedrera (La Pedrera-Casa Milà official website) (opens in a new tab)
    2. The hanging model. He found forms with hanging models: loaded threads hang in tension, and turned upside down the same curves work in compression, as arches and vaults. Sourced[1] The Gaudí Crypt: the laboratory of Sagrada Família, Colònia Güell - Cripta Gaudí (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    3. Columns on the load lines. In the Sagrada Família the columns leave the vertical and lean to follow the load lines: the line a string weighed down with a small bag of shot would draw. Sourced[1] The Sagrada Família, a repository of other Gaudí projects, Blog Sagrada Família (Basílica de la Sagrada Família) (opens in a new tab)
    4. Ruled surfaces. For the Sagrada Família he designed helicoidal piers, hyperboloid vaults and sidewalls, and a hyperbolic paraboloid roof. Sourced[1] Sagrada Família, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    5. Nature as master. Light and nature were his main sources of inspiration; he called nature his true master. Sourced[1] Gaudi obra - Antoni Gaudí - Sagrada Família, Basílica de la Sagrada Família (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    6. Trencadís. He clad surfaces in trencadís, a covering of ceramic and glass fragments, as on the façade of the Casa Batlló. Sourced[1] Gaudí’s Casa Batlló in Barcelona, Fundació Catalunya La Pedrera (La Pedrera-Casa Milà official website) (opens in a new tab)

    Works

    • 1886–1890 · Palau Güell · Barcelona
      The town palace Eusebi Güell commissioned from him: one of the first important commissions of his career. Sourced[1] Palau Güell, Palau Güell (Diputació de Barcelona, official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • 1900–1914 · Park Güell · Barcelona
      Planned as an estate of sixty plots, of which only two houses were built; the city opened it as a public park in 1923. Sourced[1] Park Güell, Gaudí's park in Barcelona, Fundació Catalunya La Pedrera (La Pedrera-Casa Milà official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • 1904–1906 · Casa Batlló · Barcelona
      A complete renovation, for the textile industrialist Josep Batlló i Casanovas, of a building put up in 1877. Sourced[1] The history | Casa Batlló, Casa Batlló (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • 1906–1912 · Casa Milà (La Pedrera) · Barcelona
      Apartments for Rosario Segimon and Pere Milà; the roof of its attic rests on a succession of 270 brick catenary arches. Sourced[1] Discover the history of La Pedrera - Casa Milà - Official website, Fundació Catalunya La Pedrera (La Pedrera-Casa Milà official website) (opens in a new tab)[2] Architecture of Gaudí’s La Pedrera | Casa Milà Official website, Fundació Catalunya La Pedrera (La Pedrera-Casa Milà official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • 1908–1914 · Crypt of the Colònia Güell · Santa Coloma de Cervelló
      Only the crypt of the planned church was built: work stopped when the Güell family withdrew its funding in 1914. Sourced[1] Gaudí and Güell: the story of the Colònia Güell, Colònia Güell - Cripta Gaudí (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • 1883–1926 · Sagrada Família · Barcelona
      When he died only the Nativity façade, one bell tower, the apse and the crypt were finished; building has gone on since. Sourced[1] Sagrada Família, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    The hologram

    The hanging model, as a method: cords in tension above a mirror, brick catenary arches below. An original illustration of his ideas, with a surface of straight lines, a branching column and broken-tile cladding, not a copy of any building. Not to scale.

  4. 04

    Frank Lloyd Wright

    1867–1959 · Prairie and modern · Architect, writer, educator

    An American architect and writer. According to Encyclopaedia Britannica, his Prairie style became the basis of 20th-century residential design in the United States. Sourced[1] Frank Lloyd Wright, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    Life & times

    • Born in Richland Center, Wisconsin, on 8 June 1867; died in Phoenix, Arizona, on 9 April 1959. Sourced[1] About Frank Lloyd Wright - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)[2] Frank Lloyd Wright, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • He was hired by Adler and Sullivan, where he worked directly under Louis Sullivan for six years. Sourced[1] About Frank Lloyd Wright - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • He designed 1,114 architectural works of all types, of which 532 were realized. Sourced[1] About Frank Lloyd Wright - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • The Robie House, completed in 1910, is described by the Frank Lloyd Wright Trust as the consummate expression of his Prairie style. Sourced[1] Frederick C. Robie House | Frank Lloyd Wright Trust, Frank Lloyd Wright Trust (opens in a new tab)
    • At Taliesin, his home near Spring Green, Wisconsin, he founded a school, the Taliesin Fellowship; in 1932 he published An Autobiography. Sourced[1] About Frank Lloyd Wright - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • He designed his first Usonian house in 1936, when the journalist Herbert Jacobs challenged him to a good house for no more than $5,000. Sourced[1] Seven Hidden Gems from Frank Lloyd Wright’s Usonian Period, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • In the late 1930s he built Taliesin West, his winter home, at the foot of the McDowell Mountains, north of Scottsdale, Arizona. Sourced[1] Frank Lloyd Wright + Arizona - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • On 7 July 2019 eight of his works were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, the first modern architecture in the United States on it. Sourced[1] In the News: Inscription of Eight Wright Buildings to UNESCO World Heritage List, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)

    “I think I first consciously tried to beat the box in the Larkin Building.”

    His lecture ‘The Destruction of the Box’, 1952, as quoted by the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation.

    Principles

    1. Organic architecture. UNESCO names its qualities: the open plan, the blurring of inside and outside, new uses of materials and technologies, and answers to each building’s setting. Sourced[1] 43COM 8B.38 - The 20th-Century Architecture of Frank Lloyd Wright (United States of America), UNESCO World Heritage Centre (opens in a new tab)
    2. Destroying the box. He set out to break open the closed box of rooms; at the Larkin Building, by his account, he pushed the stair towers out from the corners to stand free. Sourced[1] Willey House Stories: The Space Within: Part 1, "Little Boxes", Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    3. Horizontal planes. His Prairie houses stress the horizontal: the Robie House is horizontal in elevation and arranged as sliding planes in plan. Sourced[1] Frederick C. Robie House | Frank Lloyd Wright Trust, Frank Lloyd Wright Trust (opens in a new tab)
    4. Cantilevers. He projected floors and roofs as cantilevers: at Fallingwater, stacked concrete ‘trays’ reach out over the falls, each anchored to a central stone chimney mass. Sourced[1] Designing Fallingwater - Fallingwater, Fallingwater (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    5. The central hearth. A substantial fireplace, usually at the centre, is described as the heart of a Wright house; he included one in every commission. Sourced[1] Willey House Stories Part 22: Calling the Kettle Back—Chapter 6: Transcendental Flame, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    6. Compression and release. He led people from a smaller room or foyer straight into a much larger one, to heighten the sense of space. Sourced[1] Seven Hidden Gems from Frank Lloyd Wright’s Usonian Period, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)

    Works

    • 1906–1909 · Unity Temple · Oak Park, Illinois
      Visitors pass through low, dark passages he called ‘cloisters’ before rising into the open, brightly lit sanctuary. Sourced[1] Unity Temple | Frank Lloyd Wright Trust, Frank Lloyd Wright Trust (opens in a new tab)
    • 1910 · Frederick C. Robie House · Chicago, Illinois
      Its living and dining space is a single room, divided only by a central chimney. Sourced[1] Frederick C. Robie House | Frank Lloyd Wright Trust, Frank Lloyd Wright Trust (opens in a new tab)
    • 1911–1959 · Taliesin · Spring Green, Wisconsin
      His home; in the World Heritage series, the primary illustration of a building at one with its natural setting. Sourced[1] World Heritage List - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • 1936–1939 · Fallingwater · Mill Run, Pennsylvania
      Its outdoor terraces have almost the same floor area as its indoor rooms. Sourced[1] Designing Fallingwater - Fallingwater, Fallingwater (official website) (opens in a new tab)
    • 1936–1937 · Herbert and Katherine Jacobs House · Madison, Wisconsin
      In the World Heritage series, the primary illustration of how this architecture met changing ways of living at home. Sourced[1] World Heritage List - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • 1956–1959 · Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum · New York
      A helical ramp climbs from ground level to a skylight at the top. Sourced[1] Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum - Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (opens in a new tab)

    The hologram

    A closed box of rooms breaking open: free walls, sliding roof planes, a cantilevered tray, the hearth at the centre. An original diagram of his ideas, deliberately abstract, not any of his buildings. Not to scale.

  5. 05

    R. Buckminster Fuller

    1895–1983 · 20th-century modern · Engineer, architect, inventor, author

    An American engineer, architect and futurist who developed the geodesic dome. His other projects included the Dymaxion house and car, a world map projection and writings on ‘Spaceship Earth’. Sourced[1] R. Buckminster Fuller, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    Life & times

    • Born in Milton, Massachusetts, on 12 July 1895; died in Los Angeles on 1 July 1983. Sourced[1] R. Buckminster Fuller, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • He was twice expelled from Harvard University and never completed a formal education. Sourced[1] R. Buckminster Fuller, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • In 1927 the building company he was involved in ran into financial difficulties, and he, a minority stockholder, was forced out. Sourced[1] R. Buckminster Fuller, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • ‘Dymaxion’, the name of his house, car and map, joins parts of three words: dynamic, maximum and tension. Sourced[1] Dymaxion House – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    • In 1948, teaching at Black Mountain College in North Carolina, he met Kenneth Snelson. Sourced[1] Tensegrity – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    • He held 25 patents. Sourced[1] Biography – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    • Shoji Sadao, who worked with him for decades, was lead architect of the American Pavilion at Expo 67 in Montreal. Sourced[1] Shoji Sadao, Quiet Visionary – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    • In 1983, shortly before his death, he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom. Sourced[1] Biography – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)

    “Now there is one outstandingly important fact regarding Spaceship Earth, and that is that no instruction book came with it.”

    From his book Operating Manual for Spaceship Earth, 1969.

    Principles

    1. Geodesic geometry. A geodesic dome starts from an icosahedron projected onto a sphere; each of its twenty spherical triangles is divided into smaller ones, the struts on great circles. Sourced[1] US2682235A - Building construction - Google Patents, Google Patents (U.S. Patent 2,682,235) (opens in a new tab)
    2. Most volume, least surface. A sphere encloses the largest interior volume with the least surface area, which saves material and cost. Sourced[1] Domes – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    3. Tensegrity. ‘Tensional integrity’: separate compression members held within a continuous network of tension. Sourced[1] Tensegrity – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    4. Ephemeralization. His name for the process by which his goal, doing more with less, would be achieved. Sourced[1] Ephemeralizing Indie Publishing, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    5. Spaceship Earth. He used the phrase ‘spaceship earth’ for Earth’s living system as one integral whole. Sourced[1] Biography – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)
    6. Design science. He called his discipline for invention ‘Comprehensive Anticipatory Design Science’, based on discovering and using generalized principles. Sourced[1] Design Science – Buckminster Fuller Institute, Buckminster Fuller Institute (opens in a new tab)

    Works

    • 1927, 1945 · Dymaxion House · Wichita, Kansas

  6. 06

    Charles and Ray Eames

    1907–1978 · 1912–1988 · Mid-century modern · Designers, architects, filmmakers

    An American design partnership. From the 1940s to the 1970s, their furniture, toys, buildings, films, exhibitions and books aimed to improve society. Sourced[1] Overview - The Work of Charles and Ray Eames: A Legacy of Invention | Exhibitions - Library of Congress, Library of Congress (opens in a new tab)

    Life & times

    • Charles: born in St. Louis, Missouri, on 17 June 1907; died there on 21 August 1978. Ray: born in Sacramento, California, on 15 December 1912; died in Los Angeles on 21 August 1988. Sourced[1] Charles Eames and Ray Eames, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)
    • Charles studied architecture at Washington University in St. Louis for two years before being asked to leave, his views thought ‘too modern’. Sourced[1] Charles & Ray Eames Biography | Eames Foundation, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • Ray studied painting with Hans Hofmann, and in 1936 was a founding member of the American Abstract Artists. Sourced[1] Charles and Ray, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • Their partnership began when Ray helped Charles and Eero Saarinen with their winning entry for the Museum of Modern Art’s 1940 Organic Design competition. Sourced[1] Charles & Ray Eames Biography | Eames Foundation, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • They married in 1941 and moved to Los Angeles as the city prepared for the Second World War. Sourced[1] Biography - The Work of Charles and Ray Eames: A Legacy of Invention | Exhibitions - Library of Congress, Library of Congress (opens in a new tab)
    • With funding from the U.S. Navy, they and a small staff made molded plywood leg splints. Sourced[1] Charles & Ray Biography, Eames Office (opens in a new tab)
    • From 1943 to 1988 the Eames Office worked from a renovated garage at 901 Washington Boulevard, in Venice, Los Angeles. Sourced[1] Biography - The Work of Charles and Ray Eames: A Legacy of Invention | Exhibitions - Library of Congress, Library of Congress (opens in a new tab)
    • The work was joint, but Charles was often given sole credit for it. Sourced[1] Charles Eames and Ray Eames, Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

    “a very good, thoughtful host anticipating the needs of his guests”

    Charles Eames on the role of the designer, as quoted by the Eames Foundation.

    Principles

    1. Molded plywood. Plywood molded under heat and pressure into curved forms: first for U.S. Navy splints, stretchers and glider shells in the Second World War, then for chairs. Sourced[1] Eames Molded Plywood Design Story - Side Chair - Herman Miller, Herman Miller (opens in a new tab)
    2. A kit of parts. The Eames House was built from prefabricated materials ordered from industrial and commercial catalogs. Sourced[1] Case Study House #8 | Eames Foundation, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    3. The design diagram. Their design diagram shows the area where the needs and interests of the client, the design office and society as a whole overlap. Sourced[1] Eames Design Diagram Print - Eames Office, Eames Office (opens in a new tab)
    4. Guest and host. For them, being a good host meant anticipating the needs of guests; Charles described the designer’s role in the same terms. Sourced[1] About Being a Good Host, Vitra (opens in a new tab)
    5. Discipline and play. They approached problem solving as an adventure that combined discipline with a sense of play. Sourced[1] Charles and Ray, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    6. Orders of magnitude. Their film Powers of Ten explains relative size by moving the camera ten times farther away every ten seconds. Sourced[1] Powers of Ten [National Film Registry essay], by Eric Schuldenfrei, National Film Preservation Board, Library of Congress (opens in a new tab)

    Works

    • 1942–1943 · Molded Plywood Leg Splint · Los Angeles
      Made because metal splints caused further injuries through vibration in transport; an estimated 150,000 by the end of the war. Sourced[1] Molded Plywood Leg Splint | Eames Foundation, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • 1945–1946 · LCW and DCW · Los Angeles
      Molded plywood chairs, shown at the Museum of Modern Art in June 1946 as ‘New Furniture Designed by Charles Eames’. Sourced[1] MOLDED PLYWOOD GROUP - Eames Office, Eames Office (opens in a new tab)
    • 1949 · Eames House (Case Study House No. 8) · Pacific Palisades, Los Angeles
      A house and separate studio for Arts & Architecture magazine’s Case Study House program; they moved in on Christmas Eve 1949. Sourced[1] Case Study House #8 | Eames Foundation, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • 1952 · House of Cards · Los Angeles
      A construction toy of illustrated cards that slot together; each card has six slots, two on either side and one at each end. Sourced[1] Medium House of Cards - Eames Office, Eames Office (opens in a new tab)
    • 1959 · Glimpses of the U.S.A. · Moscow
      More than 2,200 images on seven screens, each thirty feet across, at the centre of the American National Exhibition. Sourced[1] Glimpses of the U.S.A. Film | Eames Foundation, Eames Foundation (opens in a new tab)
    • 1977 · Powers of Ten · Los Angeles
      The final version of a film developed from a 1963 test and a 1968 full-length version. Sourced[1] Powers of Ten [National Film Registry essay], by Eric Schuldenfrei, National Film Preservation Board, Library of Congress (opens in a new tab)

    The hologram

    Molded plywood in the making, a kit of standard parts, slotted cards, powers of ten and three overlapping areas. Original illustrations of their ideas: no chair, no house, none of their colours. Not to scale.
