01Circadian rhythms

  • The master clock follows light

    Sourced

    The suprachiasmatic nucleus in the brain is the body's master clock, and daily light reaching the eyes is the main cue that keeps it matched to the 24-hour day.

    EstablishedReferences:[1][2]

  • Hormones keep time

    Sourced

    Melatonin is made in darkness and suppressed by light, while cortisol follows a strong daily rhythm that helps time the clocks in other tissues.

    EstablishedReferences:[1][2]

  • Misalignment has costs

    Sourced

    Light at night, shift work, social jet lag and irregular sleep timing can pull daily rhythms out of alignment, and these patterns are linked to poorer metabolic health, such as reduced insulin sensitivity.

    SupportedReferences:[1]

  • Meals as a time cue

    Sourced

    Meal timing acts as a time signal for organ clocks such as the liver. Restricting eating to a daily window improved metabolic health in animals, but human evidence was sparse when the cited review was written.

    EmergingReferences:[1]

02Daily routines

  • Sleep opportunity

    Sourced

    A joint consensus of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommends that adults aged 18–60 regularly sleep 7 or more hours per night.

    EstablishedReferences:[3]

  • Bright days, dim evenings

    Sourced

    For healthy adults on daytime schedules, an expert consensus recommends bright light during the day, dim light for at least three hours before bed, and a very dark sleeping environment.

    SupportedReferences:[2]

  • Break up sitting

    Sourced

    WHO advises adults to limit sedentary time; replacing it with activity of any intensity, light activity included, benefits health.

    EstablishedReferences:[4][5]

03Weekly cycles

  • Aerobic volume across the week

    Sourced

    WHO recommends that adults aged 18–64 accumulate 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent mix, every week.

    EstablishedReferences:[4][5]

  • Muscle strengthening

    Sourced

    WHO also advises muscle-strengthening activity of at least moderate intensity, working all major muscle groups, on 2 or more days a week.

    EstablishedReferences:[4][5]

04Monthly reviews

  • Review progress

    Sourced

    Monitoring progress toward a goal modestly improved goal attainment in a meta-analysis of experimental studies, with larger effects when progress was recorded or shared with others.

    SupportedReferences:[6]

  • No proven interval

    Sourced

    Evidence does not identify a best interval for reviewing progress. TRIARC offers the monthly review as an optional reflection practice, not as an evidence-based schedule.

    InsufficientReferences:[6]

05Quarterly and annual evaluation

  • Blood pressure screening

    Sourced

    The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening adults 18 and older for high blood pressure, and confirming a diagnosis with readings taken outside the clinic before treatment starts.

    EstablishedReferences:[7]

  • Intervals depend on age and risk

    Sourced

    The task force regards yearly checks as reasonable from age 40 or with higher risk, and every 3 to 5 years for lower-risk adults aged 18–39 with a normal reading, while noting that evidence on the best interval is limited.

    SupportedReferences:[7]

  • General health checks

    Sourced

    A Cochrane review found that offering general health checks to adults not selected for disease or risk had little or no effect on deaths, and noted that such checks may lead to unnecessary tests and treatment.

    SupportedReferences:[8]

  • What this horizon is for

    Conceptual

    TRIARC uses this horizon to revisit goals and to prompt a conversation with a clinician about screening suited to age and risk. It sets no medical schedule of its own.
